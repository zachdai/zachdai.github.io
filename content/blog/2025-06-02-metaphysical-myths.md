I joined the lab late last year and was assigned the project “How to Teach Speech LLMs to Understand Multiple Languages.” However, for various reasons, there has been little substantive progress on this project to date. Although current speech LLMs possess some multilingual capabilities, their overall performance remains limited, and they generally exhibit a pronounced tendency toward English-centricity.

The biggest challenge facing speech LLMs today is the misalignment between speech and text representations. Many speech-to-text models simply use simple projection layers to map speech representations into the text embedding space, relying on supervised fine-tuning to allow the layers adjacent to this layer to adapt autonomously to the new input-output pairs. This approach is rudimentary and clumsy because speech representations are more sparse than text representations; forcing alignment results in a greater loss of text-related capabilities than of speech-related capabilities. Generally speaking, the intelligence of LLMs relies heavily on specific neural networks; even minor perturbations can cause a significant decline in their performance. After being crudely connected to a speech encoder via the method described above and fine-tuned, these models are only capable of handling very simple question-and-answer tasks. Consequently, currently open-source speech models appear rather underdeveloped; none of them offer a user experience that can match ChatGPT 3.5, a text model released quite some time ago. Simply providing a robust methodology for aligning speech and text across different languages would be sufficient to secure a paper at a prestigious conference.

## The Knowledge Distribution
However, a deeper issue with multilingual training lies in the fact that the knowledge distribution across different languages within the base model is inherently misaligned. Take LLaMA-2, whose training data is relatively open and transparent, as an example: approximately 89.70% of its corpus consists of English, while German accounts for only 0.17% and Chinese a mere 0.13%. Although non-English corpora represent a small proportion, in absolute terms, they still far exceed the total amount of reading an average person could access in a lifetime. Even if Chinese corpora account for only 0.13%, there are still 2.6 billion Chinese tokens. Even if one were to skim through them intently for eight hours a day, it would take nearly thirty years to read them all.  However, even with such a vast dataset, the linguistic capabilities of current LLMs still exhibit significant biases. Language LLMs, which are still in their early stages of development, find it particularly difficult to bridge the chasm-like gaps between different languages.

This reality has led me to ponder: Is there a metaphysical theoretical framework capable of explaining how an unknowable “black box” acquires a new language? This “black box” could be an LLM, a human being, or a human using tools. This inevitably brings to mind the famous “Chinese Room” problem in the history of philosophy, first proposed by the American philosopher John Searle in 1980. As a highly metaphysical thought experiment, the Chinese Room serves as a reductionist framework for numerous philosophical questions, much like the NP-hard problem does in computer science. Unfortunately, no convincing solution has yet been found; at the very least, the response proposed by Searle himself contains significant flaws.

## The Chinese Room: A Brief Review
Let us first briefly review the basic premise of the “Chinese Room”. This problem was proposed to cast doubt on the feasibility of strong artificial intelligence. Imagine an English-speaking native speaker who knows absolutely no Chinese, confined to a sealed room. The room has an input and output window and is equipped with three items: first, a large collection of Chinese materials, serving as a corpus; second, an English-language instruction manual detailing how to process Chinese symbols—analogous to the algorithmic rules of a program; and third, the necessary paper and writing tools for performing computational operations. When a person outside the room passes a question written in Chinese through the window, the person inside—despite having no knowledge of Chinese—can follow the manual’s instructions to compare, match, and rearrange symbols, thereby producing a new string of Chinese characters. Although these are merely mechanical operations, from an external perspective, the responses are well-formulated and semantically coherent, leading one to mistakenly believe that the person inside the room is fluent in Chinese.

Searle uses this argument to demonstrate that even if a system behaves identically to a human, it does not necessarily possess true “understanding.” The person in the Chinese Room is merely performing formalized symbolic operations, with no semantic understanding whatsoever. Therefore, even if some system can simulate human linguistic behavior, it remains “weak AI” and lacks the consciousness or understanding required for “strong AI.” This argument aims to reveal the limitations of the Turing Test: while the Turing Test focuses on behavioral manifestations, it cannot determine whether a system possesses genuine semantic understanding. In other words, if behavioral performance is the sole criterion for intelligence, then any formal system, given sufficient complexity, could “pass the test”—yet this does not imply that it possesses intrinsic cognitive abilities.

Searle’s “Chinese Room” is a rebuttal to theories that equate the mind with behavior or function; it identifies a specific scenario in which mental states are not equivalent to behavioral or functional states. But is it a problem? Behaviorists or functionalists simply do not care about this; or rather, behaviorism or functionalism is inherently incompatible with any epistemology that invokes the mind. Behaviorists and functionalists believe that it is sufficient for the entire system—comprising the person, the corpus, the manual, and pen and paper—to possess Chinese language competence. Mental states themselves may not necessarily exist; emphasizing such knowledge a priori violates Ockham’s Razor: entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity. For example, there was an article on Zhihu titled [“A Further Discussion of Transformers: The Psychotic Structure of Self-Attention and LLMs”](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/6393441749) that I found pitiable upon reading. Explaining LLMs using psychoanalysis is akin to dissecting a GPU with anatomy—it is a category mistake, in Ryle's words. Because two entities though achieving similar functions doesn't imply that they share similar structures. Only the one or two causal steps closest to the phenomena exhibit similarity. For instance, both matches and lighters can ignite a flame because ignition requires combustible material with a low ignition point and the instantaneous high temperature that is easily achieved. Both possess the conditions for ignition, and that is all. Another reason why behaviorists or functionalists do not concern themselves with actual mental states is that complex systems exhibit emergent phenomena, where the capabilities of the whole are not necessarily related to those of its parts. The “Chinese Room” scenario sidesteps—or to some extent neutralizes—these emergent phenomena, because we know a person is in it; even if that person is performing only simple computations, we might still assume that he used his intelligence. We are educated that humans are masters of their own purposes and motivations, possessing a mind independent of functional states. So, the person is the subject unrelated to phenomena that Searle forcibly introduced to argue for the dichotomy between function and mental states. This shifts the question emphasized by the Turing Test—whether a tool can serve as a cognitive agent—into whether a mind aided by a tool can achieve an independent functional agent, which appears to be an equivocation. Although the emergence has not gained wide acceptance, LLMs can be reduced to simple mathematical operations; they are products of reductionism. And once creations within a reductionist framework possess sufficient capability, even as black boxes, they are sufficient to dispel the myth of certain human-specific traits. Therefore, from a modern perspective, behaviorism or functionalism holds the upper hand, while the Chinese Room itself carries an ideological bias due to the presence of humans.

## Holes in Searle's Rebuttal
What is a human being? In Searle’s definition, humans are both irreplaceable and utterly useless. Searle refutes holism—the idea that a person carries all rules and data in their mind and functions as a single, unified whole—using the following logic: even if one memorizes the rules, one has merely committed Chinese grammar to memory, but cannot establish a meaningful connection between Chinese characters and their actual semantic meanings. However, this argument has a flaw. According to the terms of the problem, the person outside the house can only conclude that the person inside the house understands Chinese if the response is both grammatically correct and semantically appropriate. But if the person inside the house has only memorized Chinese grammar without understanding its meaning, a contradiction arises. Assuming there is no contradiction, one could conceive of a private language (English) completely unrelated to the outside world, with a set of rules allowing a person to translate this private language into a public language (Chinese). However, Wittgenstein argued that the meaning of language depends on the application of rules, and the application of rules must be verifiable. If a language is used by only one person, that person cannot distinguish between “following the rules” and “appearing to follow them,” because there is no external standard for verification. Therefore, private languages do not exist, which leads to a contradiction.

Another way to argue that Searle’s rebuttal is valid is to suggest that our understanding of grammar and semantics is flawed: as long as a language conforms to a very rigorous grammar, its semantics must necessarily be correct. However, according to Tarski’s Undecidability Theorem, in any sufficiently powerful formal system, we cannot define what constitutes “truth in the standard model of the system.” That is, a metalanguage must possess expressive power beyond that of the object language in order to express the semantics of the object language. In practice, natural languages do not meet the strict requirements of formalization. Therefore, they either cannot serve as object languages in the strict sense, or certain expressive features of natural languages must be restricted (such as excluding self-reference) in order to construct a corresponding metalanguage. Consequently, from Tarski’s perspective, there is no natural metalanguage capable of fully defining the truth of natural language. To construct such a metalanguage, one must rely on a more powerful formal system, which in itself lies beyond the expressive scope of ordinary natural language. Hence, the following syllogism holds: if there exists a set of rules that enables a person to learn a new language, and a person has mastered that set of rules, then that person has certainly learned the new language.

## Toward a Metaphysical Framework
Let us move back to the original question: How might one conceive of a metaphysical theoretical framework for language acquisition? This should be an open-ended question, because although linguistics has provided exhaustive descriptions of language structure and usage to date, it has not yet established a unified and operational metaphysical framework to explain the ontological premises and conditions of language acquisition and comprehension. Nevertheless, distilling some fundamental conclusions from practical experience and the philosophy of language can still offer valuable insights for constructing such a system.

First, language is clearly not a simple one-to-one mapping relationship between signs and meanings, because “rain cats and dogs” obviously does not mean “cats and dogs are falling from the sky”. This example clearly demonstrates that the meaning of language is not determined solely by the direct information carried by the sign itself. Therefore, any serious theory of the philosophy of language must distinguish between the relationship of “signifier” and “signified” and acknowledge the existence of deep structures in language that transcend literal semantics. The meaning of language often does not lie in individual signifiers themselves, but is generated within the chain of signifiers—that is, meaning arises from the differences, arrangements, and interrelationships between signs. This concept was already evident in Saussure’s structural linguistics and was further emphasized in Derrida’s deconstructivist view of language, namely that the meaning of language is not a static referent, but rather a product of a continuous process of “differance.”

Furthermore, the meaning of language is deeply embedded in cultural contexts and ideologies. Language not only conveys information but also serves to construct cultural identity and uphold social norms. Consequently, language use often carries implicit meanings, involving multiple dimensions such as symbolism, metaphor, and power relations. If learners attempt to understand language solely through logical or semantic rules while ignoring its cultural background, they will remain confined within the framework of their native language.

Moreover, from a pragmatic perspective, language is not isolated, fragmentary input but is embedded within a continuous context and history of communication. Understanding language often requires responding to, extending, or correcting the preceding context. For example, in some cultures, if a speaker inadvertently violates a politeness norm during a conversation, subsequent speakers may need to reconcile the situation by apologizing or reaffirming respect; however, such rhetorical actions may not be required—or even understood—in another culture. This demonstrates that language involves not only mastery of vocabulary and grammar but also the learning of social norms, interaction patterns, and cultural strategies.

Consequently, I have long believed that the common perception that acquiring a second language as an adult is less difficult than acquiring a native language as an infant or toddler is likely a misconception. On the surface, by aligning the second language with the native language in terms of semantics and structure, learners can rapidly transfer existing knowledge into the expressive system of the new language, making their understanding and use of the new language appear effective. For example, factual knowledge such as “water boils at 100 degrees Celsius under standard atmospheric pressure” does not depend on the cultural context of a specific language during the language conversion process and is therefore relatively easy to transfer. The linguistic output resulting from this transfer process may be sufficient to lead others to believe that the learner has mastered the language. However, it must be noted that this ability is predicated on the native language framework remaining intact; it essentially constitutes a functional embedding of the new language within the native language, rather than true language acquisition.

In fact, even for human learners, in most foreign language learning practices, the context corresponding to the target language is often absent, and the cultural and pragmatic background cannot be authentically presented; consequently, the learning process is highly prone to devolving into a mechanical manipulation of grammatical rules and lexical meanings. At the same time, as a highly results-oriented discipline, computer science naturally focuses its research on measurable and optimizable objectives. Within the its rapidly evolving community, which emphasizes practical benefits, philosophical reflection is often marginalized or even overlooked.

In NLP research, there is a widespread tendency to lack a fundamental concern for the nature of language and its semantic, pragmatic, and cultural complexities. Methodologies for multilingual modeling primarily focus on alignment and parallel corpora. For example, the recently proposed *Cross-Lingual Optimization for Language Transfer in Large Language Models* is essentially an extension of the DPO algorithm framework, which adjusts the output preferences of LLMs during the fine-tuning phase by introducing parallel corpora. However, it is worth noting that these parallel corpora typically exhibit pragmatic naturalness only in one language, while the other language often sacrifices its authentic pragmatic form for the sake of alignment; this approach resembles a forced adaptation of one language to another. Even when some studies attempt to challenge English-centricity—such as Meta’s *Beyond English-Centric Multilingual Machine Translation*—their methodological motivation does not lie in truly “making the target language present,” but rather in eliminating the cumulative errors resulting from English-centric methodologies. Similarly, the paper *How do Large Language Models Handle Multilingualism?* identifies neurons strongly associated with specific languages, much like isolating external tools within the “Chinese Room” concept—revealing that imbalanced training corpora lead to an internal “subject-object dichotomy” within LLMs, indicating that LLMs themselves exhibit dangerous structural biases in language processing.

## A Necessary Convergence
From a philosophical perspective, most of this research remains at the pre-reflective level, failing to delve into deeper questions such as “why is language understanding possible” or “how does language participate in the construction of cognitive structures.” However, this phenomenon can also be viewed as a natural manifestation of the development of computer science itself—which, by its very nature, does not aim to pursue the rigor of the philosophy of language. Of course, this is not necessarily a bad thing, as the development of science and technology often gives rise to disciplinary-level emergence compared to the humanities.

Against the backdrop of NLP’s development, the divide between technology and philosophy has become increasingly pronounced; yet this does not imply that the two cannot converge at a deeper level. On the contrary, it is precisely when technological dilemmas and cognitive bottlenecks frequently arise that we increasingly need philosophical intervention to identify the areas where technology itself lacks coherence and to provide a metaphysical framework for guiding practical applications. The question of “how to teach speech LLMs to understand multiple languages” appears, on the surface, to be about optimizing a technical approach, but in reality, it touches upon the philosophical foundations of language itself and the deconstruction of understanding and cognition. We observe that current speech LLMs generally lag behind in the process of “alignment,” and this lag is not merely a mistake in architectural choice, but stems from a certain neglect of the essence of the language comprehension process, as well as a compromise with the current reality of highly imbalanced corpora. The discontinuities between different modalities and languages cannot be simply bridged by linear mappings or specific neural networks, as the linguistic functions these elements perform carry structural tensions across psychological, cultural, and interactive dimensions. A deeper issue lies in the fact that the semantic spaces underlying different languages are inherently asymmetrical. Language learning is not a one-to-one mapping, but a process of contextual embedding and the reconstruction of cognitive structures.

In this sense, Searle’s Chinese Room serves as a fitting counterexample: when we attempt to describe the generative logic of linguistic behavior solely through formal systems, what we often obtain is merely a superficial representation of meaning. Searle’s insistence on “understanding” actually implies a defense of human uniqueness. Yet this defense, on the one hand, points to an untested anthropocentric assumption; on the other, it may obscure the generative logic of the linguistic system itself—namely, the mechanism of meaning that unfolds autonomously within interactive structures, independent of the subject’s intent. The controversy surrounding the Chinese Room stems precisely from the fact that it forces us to confront a crucial metaphysical dilemma: Is understanding necessarily immanent to the individual? Or does some structure of understanding exist that transcends the subject, enabling inanimate Others to participate in human language games? This is a Wittgensteinian question, and a philosophical legacy that recurs in LLM practice. Therefore, when confronting this task, we may need to reframe the question: not “how to make the model learn language,” but “how does language become possible within the model.” This is not a problem that can be solved with more parameters or larger corpora; rather, it is an open question pointing to the very nature of language. As stated in *Philosophical Investigations*: “The meaning of a word lies in its use.” The possibility of understanding also unfolds in use, in interaction, and in a new form.

Perhaps we must acknowledge that the linguistic capabilities of artificial intelligence can never become a perfect mirror image of “human language,” because humans have never been reduced to a set of rules and a symbolic system. However, this does not mean that LLM learning is futile; rather, it indicates that we are in the midst of the emergence of a new cognitive architecture that no longer takes humans as its sole reference point but attempts to use the generative logic of language itself as a coordinate system—a language metaphysics that is more emergent, less anthropocentric, and more focused on structural continuity. Thus, the boundaries of technology are the starting point of philosophy. The dilemma of speech LLMs is not merely an engineering problem but an epistemological one. And within every failed model, we may glimpse a certain outline of the essence of language. Language modeling should perhaps not be about modeling language itself, but rather about exploring the very possibility of modeling language. In this process of construction, the value of philosophy lies not in answering specific questions, but in making the questions truly meaningful.
